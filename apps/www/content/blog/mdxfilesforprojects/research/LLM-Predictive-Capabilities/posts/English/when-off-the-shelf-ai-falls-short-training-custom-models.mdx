# When Off-the-Shelf AI Falls Short: Training Custom Models

Most general AI models are incredibly capable—but not at *everything*. When you ask them to perform a task they weren’t trained on, or to reason over data they’ve never seen, performance drops. That doesn’t mean AI “can’t” do the job; it often means you need a **custom model** trained for your specific domain and task.

This post outlines what we’re researching, when custom training makes sense, and how we evaluate whether it’s technologically feasible *and* economically reasonable.

## Why custom training?

Some tasks demand **very specific data** or **tightly defined behavior**:

- Domain jargon and edge cases (e.g., legal clauses, medical device specs, internal process language)
- Private or proprietary datasets that never appear in public training corpora
- Strict output formats or compliance rules
- Latency or cost constraints at scale

In these scenarios, tailoring a model to your use case can outperform a generic model—or match its quality with **lower cost and faster inference**.

## What “custom” can mean

“Custom” isn’t one thing. We select the lightest-weight method that meets the goal:

- **Prompt & system design** — Improve instructions, structure outputs, constrain formats.
- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** — Keep your data outside the model; retrieve it at runtime.
- **Fine-tuning / adapters (LoRA, etc.)** — Teach a base model your style, data, and edge cases.
- **Distillation / compression** — Transfer behavior from a large model to a smaller, cheaper one.
- **Task-specific small models** — Train narrow models for classification, routing, or extraction.

Often, a **hybrid** (e.g., RAG + light fine-tuning) delivers the best quality-to-cost ratio.

## Our research program: technology and economics

We’re not trying to set records. We’re investigating **practical wins**: places where a custom approach is both achievable and worth it.

1. **Use-case selection**  
   We look for tasks with clear value (error reduction, time saved, conversion lift) and measurable definitions of “good.”

2. **Data strategy**  
   Identify what data is needed, how to obtain it safely, and how to label it efficiently. Use synthetic data only when validated against real examples.

3. **Baselines first**  
   Start with strong prompts and RAG on a general model. If it’s good enough, stop there.

4. **Targeted customization**  
   If the baseline misses the mark (quality, latency, cost), trial fine-tuning, adapters, or smaller specialist models.

5. **Head-to-head evaluation**  
   Measure accuracy, robustness, latency, and cost per 1,000 requests—then compare against the value the task creates.

## How we decide: an evaluation frame

| Criterion           | What we look for                                        |
| ---                | ---                                                     |
| **Task fit**       | Handles domain language and edge cases                  |
| **Quality**        | Accuracy/F1, structure adherence, error types           |
| **Speed**          | P95 latency within product constraints                  |
| **Cost**           | Per-result cost at expected scale                       |
| **Robustness**     | Performance under noisy inputs/long contexts            |
| **Maintainability**| Update effort when data or policy changes               |
| **Privacy & safety** | Data handling, access controls, auditability         |

A custom model “wins” if it improves quality **or** hits the same quality with **lower latency/cost**—and remains maintainable.

## Example project pattern (simplified)

1. **Problem**: Extract structured fields from industry-specific documents with high terminology variance.  
2. **Baseline**: General model + strict prompts → decent accuracy, too slow/expensive at scale.  
3. **Iteration**: RAG with a compact domain index → better, still misses edge cases.  
4. **Customization**: Fine-tune a smaller model on curated examples + schema adherence.  
5. **Outcome**: Same or higher accuracy, **~60–80% lower cost** per document, **lower latency**.  
6. **Guardrails**: Validation layer and drift monitors to catch schema violations over time.

> Insight: The cheapest reliable approach is the right approach. We escalate only when data shows it’s needed.

## When you probably don’t need custom training

- Prompts + RAG already meet quality and latency goals  
- No representative data (yet)  
- The task changes weekly and would outpace a tuned model  
- Traffic volume is too low to justify the work

In those cases, we document the gap and revisit once conditions change.

## Takeaway

Custom models aren’t about novelty; they’re about **fit**. If a specialized approach can reach your target quality **faster or cheaper**, it’s worth exploring. Our research program exists to identify those cases, design minimal-viable customization, and prove value with clear measurements.

### TL;DR

- General models are great, but **not universal**.  
- Some tasks need **domain data** and **tighter behavior**.  
- We test lightweight customizations first and scale up only if needed.  
- Decisions are made on **quality, latency, cost, and maintainability**—not hype.
