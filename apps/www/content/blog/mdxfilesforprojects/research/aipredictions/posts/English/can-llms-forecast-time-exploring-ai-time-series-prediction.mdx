# Can LLMs Forecast Time? Exploring AI Time-Series Prediction

![Homescreen cover](/images/blog-images/mdxfilesforprojects/research/aipredictions/images/coverpredict.png)

As AI gets better, the questions we ask it get harder. One of the toughest: **can modern AI predict how things change over time?** Unlike classical algorithms, large language models (LLMs) are **non-deterministic**, which raises concerns for time-series forecasting where stability and calibration matter. Still, with rapidly improving models and tooling, it seems less a question of *if* and more of *when* credible AI time-series forecasts become routine.

## Why time-series forecasting matters

Potential use cases span consumer, enterprise, and science:

- Markets: stock signals, regime shifts, risk alerts  
- Product & growth: user base projections, churn/retention, demand planning  
- Operations: capacity, staffing, logistics, inventory  
- Earth system science: weather nowcasting, **temperature forecasting**

Each domain has different tolerance for risk and error, which is why measuring **both accuracy and reliability** is essential.

## Our research focus

We investigate **how well transformer-based LLMs can predict variables over time** when given structured signals (e.g., forcing variables) and **semantic context** (meaningful labels/prompts). In short: does “meaning” in the input help a model reason about the future rather than just fit patterns?

Key questions:

- What’s the delta between **raw numeric inputs** vs. **semantically labeled** inputs?  
- How much does **model size** matter for forecasting skill?  
- Do **lightweight fine-tuning** approaches (e.g., QLoRA) improve stability and accuracy?  
- How do we evaluate **both** point accuracy and error tails (outliers)?

## Study spotlight: From Tokens to Temperatures (summary)

**Context.** This thesis examines whether adding semantic context improves LLM forecasting of **global mean surface air temperature** from climate forcing data.  
**Models.** Open-source Qwen-2.5 transformers at **3B** and **7B** parameters.  
**Design.** Inputs under four conditions of increasing semantic richness: (1) no labels, (2) incorrect/mismatched labels, (3) correct labels, (4) correct labels + a short explanatory prompt. Each model was evaluated **zero-shot** and after **light fine-tuning** (2 epochs, QLoRA).

**Main findings (in plain language):**

- **Meaning matters—substantially.** A 3B model rose from ~**1%** accuracy (unlabeled) to ~**29%** (correct labels). The 7B model rose from ~**10%** to ~**37%** under the same shift. Even mismatched labels beat no labels; **truthful labels** yielded the largest gains.  
- **Scale helps, context helps more.** Bigger models performed better on average, yet the **largest jumps** came from adding **semantic context**.  
- **Light fine-tuning boosts stability and accuracy.** The **7B** model reached **~45%** best accuracy post fine-tuning, and **large outliers (>5 K)** were virtually eliminated whenever labels were present.  
- **Statistical significance.** ANOVA and post-hoc tests confirm that improvements from **semantic context**, **model size**, and **fine-tuning** are significant.

## Why this matters

These results provide controlled evidence that LLMs can use **semantic information**—not only numeric correlations—to make **more accurate and more stable** scientific forecasts. With careful problem framing (labels/prompts) and modest adaptation (light fine-tuning), LLMs move closer to genuine data understanding in complex time-series tasks.

> Full paper: [From Tokens to Temperatures (Google Doc)](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1C_aoIrUVfBRAkgEE1aik6_ByQjDdnZfZxJs4OQDMs5Q/edit?usp=sharing)

## Takeaways

- LLMs are stochastic, but **stochastic ≠ unusable** for forecasting.  
- **Semantic labels are leverage**—they improve both accuracy and error behavior, even for small models.  
- Evaluate **error tails**, not just mean scores; eliminating large outliers can matter most in practice.

## What’s next

We’re testing generalization beyond climate (user metrics, ops signals, financial series), comparing LLMs with classical forecasters under identical data regimes, and measuring **calibration, uncertainty, drift**, and **cost–quality trade-offs** for production viability.
